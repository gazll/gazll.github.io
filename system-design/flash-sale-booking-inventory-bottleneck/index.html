Flash-sale booking and inventory: finding and removing the hot-stock bottleneck

The pictured architecture serves one million daily users and roughly ten times normal promotional traffic, but the dangerous number is not daily users or total requests. Product pages multiply reads into inventory checks, while every buyer of one discounted SKU contends on the same stock record or Redis lock. The initial Book → Inventory → Payment chain couples failures: admission arrives too late, a payment command can be lost after a hold commits, and scaling pods cannot raise one hot key's serial capacity. This design makes the bottleneck measurable, gives Inventory one authoritative decision, and operates explicit low-traffic, pre-peak, surge, protection and recovery modes so each component is pre-warmed, autoscaled, capped or scaled in safely.

Scope and problem framing

The pictured architecture serves one million daily users and roughly ten times normal promotional traffic, but the dangerous number is not daily users or total requests.

Product pages multiply reads into inventory checks, while every buyer of one discounted SKU contends on the same stock record or Redis lock.

  • The initial Book → Inventory → Payment chain couples failures: admission arrives too late, a payment command can be lost after a hold commits, and scaling pods cannot raise one hot key's serial capacity.
  • This design makes the bottleneck measurable, gives Inventory one authoritative decision, and operates explicit low-traffic, pre-peak, surge, protection and recovery modes so each component is pre-warmed, autoscaled, capped or scaled in safely.

Requirements

Functional requirements

  • Serve product/search availability from a versioned projection; revalidate only when creating an authoritative hold.
  • Admission control:Admit a bounded number of checkout attempts per campaign/SKU and return sold-out, queued or retry-after responses before the inventory authority saturates.
  • Authoritative hold:Create an idempotent hold with quantity, fencing version and expiry using one atomic conditional write; never keep a database transaction open while calling payment.
  • Booking saga:Run authorize/capture and order confirmation as a durable saga, release expired or failed holds and reconcile unknown PSP outcomes.
  • Client-facing state:Expose campaign state, queue position and truthful pending/sold-out results so clients do not poll inventory aggressively.
  • Durable handoff:Commit stock, expiring hold and outbox together; emit hold-created/payment work only after commit, then return confirm-or-release commands to the Inventory authority.
  • Traffic lifecycle:Operate NORMAL, PRE_PEAK, SURGE, PROTECT and RECOVERY policies with versioned admission, DB/PSP budgets, user response and rollback owner.
  • Backpressure contract:Stop or slow producer work before the scarce boundary with bounded admission, in-flight holds, consumer pull/prefetch, deadlines, cancellation, Retry-After and load shedding; never hide overload in an unbounded queue.

Quality attributes

  • Inventory invariant:Confirmed plus active-held quantity never exceeds sellable stock; Redis cache or lock loss cannot violate this invariant.
  • Overload isolation:Search remains available during checkout overload, and overload for one campaign/SKU cannot consume every DB connection or worker.
  • Idempotency boundary:The API is idempotent across client retry, gateway retry, queue redelivery and payment callback duplication.
  • Admission deadline:p99 hold latency stays within the admission deadline; once the queue cannot drain inside the business window, reject rather than hide demand in an unbounded backlog.
  • Audit trail:Every hold, expiry, payment and compensation transition is auditable and can be replayed or reconciled without mutating history.
  • Scale-in safety:A worker drains or checkpoints its current message before termination; scale-in cannot strand an active hold, unpublished outbox event or unknown payment.
  • Budget isolation:Browse, batch and analytics work have bounded DB/PSP concurrency so a scale-out in one path cannot consume the reservation budget.
  • Backpressure safety:When arrival rate exceeds drain rate, queue age and deadline misses stay bounded by rejecting, shedding or slowing work before DB connections, locks and PSP concurrency are exhausted.

Capacity and constraints

  • Baseline:1M DAU does not imply high throughput—uniform traffic is only 11.6 user arrivals/s, and the everyday planning figure is 250 rps peak (1M req/day). The launch window is a tier jump, not a peak: 50k users refreshing five times in 60 seconds produces about 4,167 edge requests/s before images and retries.
  • Read amplification:if a page checks 20 SKUs separately, 4,000 page requests/s becomes 80,000 inventory lookups/s. Batch the read, serve a denormalized projection/cache, and remove authoritative inventory from the browse path.
  • Hot-SKU serialization:capacity is approximately 1 / critical-section time. A 12 ms atomic reservation has a theoretical ceiling near 83 reservations/s for one serialized key; operate around 60–70% (about 50–58/s) to leave failover and variance headroom.
  • Hot-key slowdown:If lock/transaction time rises to 25 ms under WAL, replication or index pressure, the same SKU falls to 40/s theoretical and roughly 24–28/s safe. Ten application replicas do not raise this per-key limit; they only create more waiters.
  • Connection bound:120 usable DB connections with 40 ms average transaction time suggests 3,000 tx/s only when work is independent. One hot row remains limited by its serial service time; if synchronous payment holds each connection for 0.8 s, the same pool has a theoretical ceiling of only 150 checkout/s.
  • Example admission:benchmark p99 atomic hold at 12 ms, choose 50 hold attempts/s per hot SKU, cap the waiting room at 10 seconds (500 admitted attempts), and reject/mark sold-out beyond it. These are planning numbers, not product guarantees.
  • Production limit:Find the real knee with a production-shaped pgbench/custom load test: Poisson arrivals plus a flash burst, realistic hot-SKU skew, WAL/replica settings and indexes. Increase target rate until schedule lag, p99 lock wait, serialization retry or error budget rises non-linearly; set the production limit below that point.
  • Capacity SLOs:DB pool below 70%, p99 lock wait below 20 ms, hold success response below 250 ms, post-admission backend queue age below 2 s, payment pending age bounded by provider SLA, and zero negative inventory.
  • Autoscaler gap:if a fleet needs 90 seconds to start and safe admission is 50/s while a campaign sends 2,000/s, an unbounded queue grows by roughly 175,500 requests before warm-up completes. Pre-warm known events; use the waiting room to bound unknown spikes.
  • Scaling vector:CDN, cache, search, gateways and independent workers scale with replicas; a viral SKU remains bounded by its 1 / critical-section rate. Measure and set a separate per-SKU admission cap rather than treating total RPS as capacity.
  • Scale-in gate:reduce capacity only after no active campaign, queue/outbox age is inside SLO, payment unknown and expiry backlog are draining, and p99/pool/lock signals are stable through a cooldown window.
  • Backpressure capacity check:admit only when drain_rate exceeds arrival_rate and estimated_drain_time fits the business window; otherwise cap the waiting room, shed low-priority work or return Retry-After instead of growing backlog.
  • Judge the pre-reservation layer per tier, because a flash sale is a tier jump rather than a traffic level: not needed at T1 and T2, where the conditional decrement alone absorbs the contention; mandatory at T3, since a sale concentrates a day of demand into minutes and the database is asked to serialize all of it; needs its own scaling story at T4, where the counter itself is sharded and the reconciliation between shard counters and the durable record becomes a component with an owner.

Data model

Compare records by ownership and responsibility before choosing storage. A record is authoritative, operational or derived; mixing those roles creates unclear consistency boundaries.

Record / modelFields, ownership and responsibility

01Campaign/SKU policy

campaign_id, sku_id, sellable quota, per-user limit, admission rate, burst, start/end time and policy version.

02Inventory position

sku/warehouse partition key, on_hand, reserved, sold, safety stock, monotonic version and last fencing token; enforce reserved + sold ≤ sellable in the authoritative transaction.

03Hold

hold_id, sku, user, quantity, state, idempotency key/fingerprint, fencing version and expires_at; one active hold per business key where policy requires it.

04Payment/order saga

own payment intent, hold reference, provider attempt, deadline and next action; pending and unknown are explicit states and it never shares an inventory transaction.

05Reservation command

command_id, hold_id, sku_id, quantity, idempotency key, admission token and deadline; replay-safe at the booking boundary.

06Distributed lock/fencing

lock key, owner, fencing token, lease deadline and authoritative inventory version; stale owners are rejected by the writer.

07Inventory update event

event_id, sku_id, inventory_version, delta/state snapshot and occurred_at; partitioned by SKU for ordered projections.

08Payment result event

payment_intent_id, order_id, provider_attempt, outcome, idempotency key and received_at; unknown is a first-class outcome.

09Outbox/inbox

stable event ID, aggregate/version, payload schema and publish/consume status for idempotent propagation.

10Capacity policy

campaign mode, warm-up window, min/max replicas, per-SKU admission rate, DB/PSP budget, cooldown and policy version; every mode change is auditable and reversible.

11Worker drain lease

consumer, message/partition, visibility deadline, checkpoint and termination state; prevents scale-in from losing work or duplicating an external effect.

12Availability projection

campaign/SKU, approximate remaining band, version and observed_at; optimized for browse and never authoritative for confirmation.

Compare the models on
  • transaction boundary and invariant
  • read/write path and indexes
  • partition key and hot-key risk
  • retention, audit and privacy
  • whether it can be rebuilt after loss

Technology choices

Treat this as a decision map, not a shopping list. Each component must solve a named constraint and carry an explicit operational cost.

Layer / optionChoice, purpose and boundary

01Edge waiting room/CDN

absorbs synchronized refreshes and releases signed admission tokens at a rate the checkout path has measured it can sustain; its bounded queue age is the first backpressure valve; alternative is immediate 429/sold-out for very short campaigns.

02API gateway limiter

limits per campaign, SKU, account and device/API key; weighted checkout cost is more useful than IP-only request count behind NAT or bot networks.

03PostgreSQL/MySQL inventory authority

conditional UPDATE or short SELECT FOR UPDATE transaction with unique constraints; preferred when oversell is unacceptable because correctness survives cache loss.

04Redis

excellent for waiting-room counters, admission tokens, cached availability and optional atomic reservation when bounded loss/repair is explicitly accepted; a TTL lock alone is not the stock source of truth.

05Booking lock/fencing

use a short-lived lock or single-writer SKU partition only around the reservation decision; attach a monotonically increasing fencing token so a paused owner cannot write after lease expiry.

06Inventory-update MQ

publish the committed stock/hold change through a transactional outbox; partition by SKU, process at least once, dedupe by event ID/version and route poison messages to a visible DLQ.

07Payment-result MQ

PSP adapters publish authorized, failed, canceled or unknown results; consumers advance the order state idempotently and reconcile unknown outcomes before release/refund.

08Payment guard

use idempotency key + unique payment intent + versioned state transition as the payment lock; never hold the inventory lock or DB transaction while waiting for PSP.

09Kafka/RabbitMQ plus transactional outbox

notification, projection and analytics leave the reservation transaction; consumers are idempotent and expose oldest-message age and DLQ ownership.

10Durable workflow/orchestrator

owns hold → payment → confirm/release deadlines and status queries; cron-only compensation is simpler but reacts slowly and hides per-order progress.

11Scheduled pre-warm

for predictable campaigns, raise the critical path's minimum replicas, cache working set and connection budget before the start time; measure startup/warm-up rather than assuming reactive autoscaling is instant.

12Per-component autoscaling

scale UI/API from concurrency or requests/target, queue workers from oldest-message age plus per-partition lag, and payment from pending age; cap each by its DB/PSP downstream budget.

13Drain-first scale-in

remove only idle/stateless capacity after queue/outbox/payment/expiry gates are healthy; workers stop pulling, finish or checkpoint, then release their lease before termination.

14Cost tiers

analytics, reindex and bulk notifications may scale to zero; checkout API/payment floor, inventory authority, outbox, expiry, callback receiver and HA data stores must retain a minimum safe capacity.

15Observability

trace admission-to-hold-to-payment, measure per-SKU lock wait/attempt/success without SKU as an unbounded metric label, and log sampled high-cardinality campaign/SKU details.

Challenge every choice with
  • simplest viable alternative
  • consistency and latency impact
  • failure and degraded mode
  • team/on-call capability
  • migration and exit trigger

Trade-offs

Each row is a tension the design accepts—not a universal best practice. Review both sides and record the condition that would reverse the decision.

01
Synchronous boundary should end after the authoritative hold decision

admission, booking and inventory write return held/queued/sold-out. Payment authorization and result delivery cross an asynchronous saga boundary, so the API can return pending without holding a connection.

02
Booking lock/fencing protects the short concurrent…

Booking lock/fencing protects the short concurrent write, but a distributed lock must never span the PSP call. Payment uses an idempotency guard and versioned state machine instead; otherwise a slow PSP turns lock expiry into double-spend or oversell risk.

03
Inventory-update and payment-result MQs improve isolation…

Inventory-update and payment-result MQs improve isolation but are at-least-once. Ordering is per aggregate/SKU, not global; require event IDs, inbox dedupe, retry with backoff, DLQ ownership and reconciliation dashboards.

04
Reactive autoscaling saves steady-state cost but…

Reactive autoscaling saves steady-state cost but acts after measurement, scheduling and warm-up. Scheduled pre-warm costs idle capacity yet is the only reliable response for a campaign whose launch minute is already known.

05
Scale-out must use a component's own proportional signal

CPU/request concurrency for stateless APIs, oldest event age and partition skew for workers, and DB/PSP budgets as hard caps. A single global CPU target hides the first saturated dependency.

06
Scale-to-zero is attractive for analytics, reindex…

Scale-to-zero is attractive for analytics, reindex and bulk notification, but wrong for the inventory authority, hold expiry, outbox relay, PSP callback and reconciliation paths. Their idle cost buys correctness and a bounded recovery time.

07
The original shared Book+Payment database minimizes…

The original shared Book+Payment database minimizes early hops but couples deployments, pools and failures. Separate Inventory/Hold ownership from Payment/Order saga ownership; connect them with outbox events rather than an implicit cross-DB transaction.

08
A cache or read replica removes…

A cache or read replica removes browse load, not a hot inventory write. A quota bucket/cell or single-writer lane can isolate a viral SKU, but adds stranded quota, fairness and reconciliation costs.

09
Synchronous payment gives a final response…

Synchronous payment gives a final response but holds threads, connections and possibly stock during the slowest dependency. A saga returns pending sooner and adds idempotency, reconciliation and a more complex user contract.

10
Database conditional updates give the clearest…

Database conditional updates give the clearest invariant and a per-hot-key serial ceiling. Redis atomic scripts are faster but move durability, failover and repair assumptions into application correctness.

11
A waiting room protects the system…

A waiting room protects the system and improves fairness, but exact queue position is expensive and misleading under abandonment; return a band or estimated wait instead.

12
A longer hold TTL improves checkout…

A longer hold TTL improves checkout completion while reducing inventory utilization and enabling hoarding. Use a short base TTL, per-user caps and policy-controlled extension after payment authorization begins.

13
Partitioning by SKU spreads different products…

Partitioning by SKU spreads different products but never scales one viral SKU. For that key, reduce critical-section time, pre-split a quota into fenced buckets or serialize commands in one partition and accept bounded throughput.

14
Pre-allocating stock to cells/regions removes a…

Pre-allocating stock to cells/regions removes a global write hop and contains failure, but stranded quota can show sold-out in one cell while another has stock; rebalance only through fenced quota transfer.

15
Queueing/backpressure smooths a burst only when…

Queueing/backpressure smooths a burst only when drain rate exceeds sustainable arrivals soon enough.

  • If 2,000 attempts/s enter a 50/s hot-SKU worker, backlog grows 1,950/s
  • after ten seconds it already needs about 390 seconds to drain, so bounded admission and rejection are the honest response.
16
Failure review

test Redis loss, DB primary failover, an expired hold racing with payment success, duplicated payment callbacks, outbox lag, bot-distributed identities and a client retry storm after a gateway timeout.

Failure review — answers for this design
01

What invariant can break, and what keeps it true?

The only non-negotiable invariant is confirmed plus active-held quantity never exceeding sellable stock.

  • Inventory DB is the write authority: one conditional reservation write commits position, hold and outbox together; cache, Redis lock and projections are never allowed to confirm stock.
  • If reconciliation detects a mismatch, freeze admission for that SKU and repair from immutable hold, order and PSP evidence rather than editing the stock counter by hand.
02

Which signal detects the incident before customers see a collapse?

Alert on the first saturated boundary, not aggregate CPU: p99 inventory lock wait above 20 ms, hold latency above 250 ms, usable DB pool above 70%, post-admission queue age above 2 s, hot-partition lag, PSP unknown age and attempt-to-original retry ratio. The campaign dashboard must show these per campaign and sampled hot SKU, with a named owner who can move the mode to PROTECT.

03

How is the blast radius contained during a hot SKU or dependency failure?

Admission is capped per campaign/SKU before Book and Inventory; checkout has bounded DB and PSP concurrency separate from browse, batch and analytics.

  • In PROTECT, lower the SKU admission rate, stop optional work, serve cached approximate availability and return queued, retry-after or sold-out instead of accumulating an unbounded backend queue.
  • Search stays available because it never calls the authoritative stock write path.
04

Can retries duplicate, reorder or incorrectly finalize work?

A client idempotency key maps to one hold, payment intent and terminal response; event IDs plus inbox dedupe make MQ redelivery harmless.

  • Ordering is only per SKU/aggregate, so every state transition checks its expected version and a stale confirm/release is rejected.
  • PSP timeout becomes PAYMENT_UNKNOWN: query and reconcile the provider before capture retry, release or refund; never use a Redis lease as proof that a payment may be repeated.
05

How do we prove recovery after Redis loss, DB failover or a partial saga?

Rebuild projections from the inventory outbox, replay idempotently by aggregate/version, and compare every active hold with payment/order saga and PSP records.

  • The expiry worker reconciles old holds, while a dedicated unknown-payment job queries the PSP and emits one fenced confirm, release or refund decision.
  • Recovery is complete only when the reconciliation diff is zero, queue/outbox and expiry age return inside SLO, and the audit trail can explain each stock transition.

Engineering deep dives

Flash-sale capacity and hot-key contention

A campaign is an open-loop burst against one scarce key. Measure the serialized reservation path, then admit no faster than it can safely drain.

Find the knee, not a vendor TPS

  • Replay the real reservation transaction with hot-key skew and target-rate arrivals. Track schedule lag, lock wait, deadlock/serialization retry, WAL latency and pool occupancy together.
  • The first nonlinear rise in p99 or schedule lag is the saturation knee. Keep production admission below it with headroom; rerun after schema, index, hardware or durability changes.

Separate popularity from correctness

  • Browse reads use a cached projection and may show an availability band. Only the hold command touches authoritative stock, so a refresh storm cannot become a row-lock storm.
  • A Redis reservation script is atomic inside Redis but still needs an explicit durability/recovery contract. For zero-oversell inventory, a database invariant or fenced single-writer authority remains the final judge.

Bound demand before work starts

  • Issue admission tokens per campaign/SKU, cap concurrent holds and give the queue a maximum waiting age. Reject when estimated drain time exceeds the useful checkout window.
  • Load-test client retries and bots, not only cooperative users. A timeout without Retry-After or a truthful pending state can multiply attempts exactly when capacity is lowest.

Elastic capacity across the traffic lifecycle

Scale-out is useful only for work that can run in parallel. Known peaks need scheduled warm capacity; quiet periods need deliberate, drain-safe scale-in rather than an indiscriminate scale-to-zero policy.

Pre-warm known peaks; bound unknown spikes

  • For a scheduled campaign, raise the critical-path floor before launch using measured startup, cache warm-up and connection-pool time. Reactive autoscaling begins after demand is observed, so admission control must cover the warm-up gap.
  • For surprise traffic, scale gateway, read path and stateless APIs from concurrency/request signals, but issue no more checkout work than the inventory, database and PSP budgets can safely drain.

Use one scaling signal per component

  • UI/API capacity follows request concurrency or requests per healthy target; queue consumers need oldest-message age, input/output rate and partition skew; payment workers also need a provider-concurrency cap.
  • A hot SKU, shared row, database lock or one ordered partition is a synchronization point. More replicas reduce surrounding work but do not raise that key’s serialized rate; use admission, quotas, cells or a single-writer lane instead.

Scale in after drain, not merely after CPU falls

  • Scale down only after the campaign/recovery window ends and queue, outbox, payment-pending and expiry signals are healthy through a cooldown. Stop pulling work, finish or checkpoint the current item, then release its lease before termination.
  • Scale-to-zero fits intermittent analytics, reindex and bulk notifications. Retain a safe floor for checkout, inventory authority, outbox, expiry, reconciliation, provider callback handling and observability because their idle cost buys correctness and recovery time.

Correctness across money and inventory

The database protects local invariants; idempotency, state machines and reconciliation protect the workflow around it.

Make commands repeatable

  • Persist idempotency key, request fingerprint and original response together. Reject key reuse with different parameters and define retention from the maximum retry window.
  • Represent pending and unknown outcomes explicitly. After a timeout, query authoritative provider state before retrying or compensating an irreversible action.

Keep invariants local

  • Put uniqueness, balance and legal state transitions in one transaction and enforce them with constraints, conditional updates or locks—not a cache lock.
  • Choose isolation from the anomaly to prevent. Serializable transactions can abort under contention, so the whole transaction needs a bounded retry path.

Close the distributed gap

  • Write business state and an outbox record atomically; consumers deduplicate effects. Exactly-once inside one platform does not make an external PSP or airline atomic.
  • Reconcile independent statements, retain immutable audit history and repair by compensating entries. Monitor aged pending states and unmatched totals as correctness SLIs.

Policy and distributed enforcement

A limiter protects a scarce resource; its key, cost model and failure mode matter more than the counter algorithm.

Model the budget

  • Scope by authenticated tenant/user/API key before IP where possible. Charge weighted cost for expensive queries instead of pretending every request costs one token.
  • Separate sustained rate, burst capacity and concurrency. Version policy and shadow new rules before enforcement to measure false positives.

Bound distributed error

  • Use atomic server-side updates for a precise shared bucket; use local token leases only when bounded overshoot is acceptable and measurable.
  • Choose fail-open or fail-closed per endpoint. Authentication, checkout and public reads have different abuse risk and availability cost.

Operate the control

  • Track allowed, rejected and shadow decisions by policy version plus limiter latency, hot keys and degraded-mode duration—without putting user IDs in metric labels.
  • Return a stable error contract and Retry-After where meaningful; combine rate limits with quotas, bot controls and origin concurrency protection.

Authority survives cache loss: The inventory row and hold/order state machine are authoritative. A short conditional transaction enforces confirmed + active-held ≤ sellable; every transition carries an expected version or fencing value.

Redis may rebuild waiting-room counters and availability projections, but its loss cannot alter stock.

One intent, one provider attempt: Persist the client idempotency key and request fingerprint with the hold/order, then create an outbox event. The PSP worker uses one stable provider idempotency key; a timeout is UNKNOWN, never permission to issue a second independent charge.

Webhooks, polls, and queue deliveries are recorded idempotently.

Resolve races explicitly: An expiry worker releases only the matching still-active version.

A late PSP success may not silently confirm an expired hold: reload authority, query the provider, then confirm only if policy and stock allow it; otherwise cancel/refund or compensate through a new recorded transition.

Monitor unknown-payment age, outbox lag, duplicate rates, and reconciliation debt.
