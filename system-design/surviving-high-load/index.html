Surviving overload with bounded work

Design overload behavior for a service whose incoming work can exceed CPU, connection pools, queues or downstream capacity. The goal is bounded latency and predictable degradation, not accepting every request.

Scope and problem framing

Design overload behavior for a service whose incoming work can exceed CPU, connection pools, queues or downstream capacity. The goal is bounded latency and predictable degradation, not accepting every request.

Requirements

Functional requirements

  • Classify work by tenant, endpoint, cost and business priority before admission.
  • Reject, defer or degrade excess work with a contract clients can safely follow.
  • Cancel expired work and release resources across every hop.
  • Keep serving the critical journey at a reduced feature set rather than serving every journey badly.

Quality attributes

  • Bound concurrency and queue age at the earliest boundary that knows request cost.
  • Reserve capacity for critical journeys and prevent one tenant from consuming it all.
  • Keep retries within budgets with exponential backoff, jitter and end-to-end deadlines.
  • Degradation must be a designed path that is exercised, not an emergent behaviour discovered at peak. An untested fallback is a second incident waiting inside the first.

Capacity and constraints

  • Derive safe concurrency from measured service time rather than from a round number: at ~250 rps peak (1M req/day) at T3 with a 40 ms service time, Little's Law puts the in-flight count at about 10, so a pool of 200 threads is not headroom — it is 190 requests queueing inside the process where no limiter can see them.
  • The peak factor is the input everything else depends on, so state which traffic shape you assumed: consumer and campaign traffic concentrates 10–20×, steady internal and B2B traffic 3–5×. A T3 service planned at the 11.6 rps average instead of the 250 rps peak is under-provisioned by a factor of twenty.
  • Judge admission control per tier:not needed at T1, where a single instance saturates before any queue forms; worth it at T2, where one expensive endpoint can already starve the rest; mandatory at T3, because overload arrives faster than autoscaling can respond; needs its own scaling story at T4, where per-tenant fairness and priority classes each become their own scheduling problem.
  • Include startup lag before treating autoscaling as an overload response. A pod that takes 90 seconds to become ready cannot answer a spike that arrives in 10, so headroom or pre-scaling is what covers a known event and autoscaling covers the trend behind it.
  • Budget retry amplification as a multiplier on the peak:three attempts turn a 250 rps peak into 750 rps against a dependency that is already failing, which is why the retry budget must be a global share of traffic and not only a per-call setting.
  • Load-test step spikes, ramps, slow dependencies, queue saturation and recovery — and measure recovery separately, because a system that sheds correctly and then takes twenty minutes to drain has only moved the outage.

Data model

Compare records by ownership and responsibility before choosing storage. A record is authoritative, operational or derived; mixing those roles creates unclear consistency boundaries.

Record / modelFields, ownership and responsibility

01Admission policy

scope, limit, priority class, cost weight and enforcement mode. Cost weight matters because one report export is not one page view, and counting requests treats them as if it were.

02Request budget

deadline, retry count, idempotency key and cancellation state, propagated across every hop so that a hop which has already missed the deadline does no work at all.

03Overload event

reason, rejected class, saturation signal and recovery timestamp. The reason is what separates "we shed deliberately" from "we fell over", and only one of those is a success.

04Capacity envelope

measured service time, safe concurrency and the observed peak factor per endpoint, stored so that a limit has a derivation rather than a history of nudges.

Compare the models on
  • transaction boundary and invariant
  • read/write path and indexes
  • partition key and hot-key risk
  • retention, audit and privacy
  • whether it can be rebuilt after loss

Technology choices

Treat this as a decision map, not a shopping list. Each component must solve a named constraint and carry an explicit operational cost.

Layer / optionChoice, purpose and boundary

01Gateway and service limiter for tenant and endpoint policy, plus a concurrency limiter near every scarce pool

connections, threads, downstream slots. The limiter belongs where the scarcity is, not only at the front door.

02Bounded queues everywhere, with depth and oldest-message age both exposed. Depth alone hides the failure that matters

a queue that is short but old is starving, and a queue that is long but young is merely busy.

03Deadline propagation from the edge inward,…

Deadline propagation from the edge inward, so that expired work is cancelled rather than completed for a client that has already given up. Work done after the deadline is pure loss and it competes with work that still matters.

04Priority classes as separate pools rather…

Priority classes as separate pools rather than a priority field on one queue, because a field lets a single consumer pool starve a class while the pool itself looks healthy.

05Adaptive concurrency limits

additive increase, multiplicative decrease against observed latency — with hard bounds either side. A control loop without bounds is an outage generator with a feedback term.

06Observability

admitted versus rejected by class, saturation signals per pool, deadline expiry rate, retry budget consumption, queue oldest-age, and time-to-drain after a shed event.

Challenge every choice with
  • simplest viable alternative
  • consistency and latency impact
  • failure and degraded mode
  • team/on-call capability
  • migration and exit trigger

Code samples

Concurrency from measurement, not from a round number

Little's Law is the derivation a pool size should have. Sizing a pool at 200 because it looked safe is how 190 requests end up queueing invisibly inside the process.

java
// L = arrival rate x service time
//   250 rps peak x 0.040 s = 10 concurrent requests at the design peak
//
// so a bulkhead of ~12 (10 plus headroom) is the real limit. A 200-thread pool
// does not admit more work usefully; it hides 190 requests behind the limiter
// where queue age is invisible and the deadline expires unnoticed.

Bulkhead bulkhead = Bulkhead.of("checkout", BulkheadConfig.custom()
        .maxConcurrentCalls(12)
        .maxWaitDuration(Duration.ofMillis(50))   // fail fast: a long wait IS the outage
        .build());

Response handle(Request request) {
    if (request.deadline().isBefore(clock.instant())) {
        return Response.gone();          // the client already gave up; doing the work is pure loss
    }
    return bulkhead.executeSupplier(() -> service.handle(request));
}

Shed by class, and say so

The two signals that matter are separated: oldest-age says a class is starving, depth alone does not. A refusal carries a retry hint so clients back off instead of amplifying.

bash
# what a healthy shed looks like in metrics
admitted_total{class="checkout"}      1240   # critical: still fully served
admitted_total{class="reporting"}       12   # non-critical: shed deliberately
rejected_total{class="reporting",reason="admission_policy"}  4180

# the signal that a class is starving, which queue depth alone hides
queue_oldest_age_seconds{class="checkout"}    0.4
queue_oldest_age_seconds{class="reporting"}  92.0

# and the refusal the client receives — a bare 503 makes it retry immediately
HTTP/1.1 503 Service Unavailable
Retry-After: 30
X-Shed-Class: reporting

Trade-offs

Each row is a tension the design accepts—not a universal best practice. Review both sides and record the condition that would reverse the decision.

01
A large queue increases apparent acceptance…

A large queue increases apparent acceptance while converting overload into unbounded latency. The honest choice is a short queue and a fast refusal, because a client that is told no can do something about it and a client that is kept waiting cannot.

02
Fail-open preserves availability and can destroy…

Fail-open preserves availability and can destroy the dependency the control was protecting; fail-closed protects the dependency and turns a control-plane outage into a full one. Choose per control, and write the choice down.

03
Adaptive limits track workload cost and…

Adaptive limits track workload cost and are control loops that oscillate without hard bounds. A static limit derived from measurement is often better than an adaptive one nobody has tuned.

04
Shedding by priority protects the business…

Shedding by priority protects the business and requires the request to be classified before admission, which means the classification itself must be cheap. If deciding a request's priority costs as much as serving it, the shed has bought nothing.

05
Autoscaling adds capacity after detection and…

Autoscaling adds capacity after detection and warmup, so it answers a trend and not a spike. Provisioning for the peak wastes money continuously; provisioning for the average outsources the peak to your users.

06
Every degradation path is code that…

Every degradation path is code that only runs during an incident, which makes it the least tested code in the system. Exercising it in production on a schedule is the only way it is real.

Failure review — answers for this design
01

Which invariant can break?

The critical journey must keep its reserved capacity no matter what the rest of the traffic does, and admitted work must complete inside its deadline or be cancelled. Both break the same way: a shared pool with a priority field instead of separate pools, where one class quietly consumes the capacity another was promised.

02

Which metric detects it first?

Queue oldest-age per class, not queue depth and not average latency. A starving class shows as its oldest-age climbing while depth and aggregate latency look normal, because the requests that are being served fast are the ones being counted.

03

How is the blast radius contained?

Priority classes are separate pools with reserved capacity, concurrency limiters sit next to each scarce resource rather than only at the edge, retry budgets are capped globally, and deadlines cancel work that has already lost its client.

04

Can retry duplicate or reorder work?

Yes, and under load it is the amplifier that turns a slow dependency into a dead one. Retries need a global budget, exponential backoff with jitter, and an idempotency key so that the duplicate a retry creates is harmless when it does arrive.

05

How do we repair and prove recovery?

Restore capacity, then let queues drain under the same admission policy rather than lifting the limits — lifting them is what produces a second collapse on the backlog. Recovery is proven when oldest-age is back inside budget for every class, the reject rate returns to baseline, and time-to-drain matches what the load test predicted.

Engineering deep dives

Reliability, overload and recovery

Reliability is a user-visible contract. Capacity, retries and failover must be designed as bounded control loops rather than emergency switches.

Prevent positive feedback

  • Give every call an end-to-end deadline; derive shorter per-hop timeouts from the remaining budget and cancel work the caller no longer needs.
  • Retry only classified transient failures, cap attempts, add exponential backoff with jitter and enforce a retry budget so recovery traffic cannot become the outage.

Degrade deliberately

  • Use admission control, bounded queues, concurrency limits and load shedding before saturation; queue depth without oldest-age is not enough to see stalled work.
  • Define which features fail closed, fail open, serve stale data or become read-only. Test the degraded contract with dependency latency, partial-region loss and bad configuration.

Prove recovery

  • Set RTO and RPO per journey, automate restore/failover where safe, and regularly restore production-shaped backups into an isolated environment.
  • Roll out by canary or cell, compare user SLIs, and keep rollback independent of the failing control plane. A failover plan that has never been exercised is an assumption.

Policy and distributed enforcement

A limiter protects a scarce resource; its key, cost model and failure mode matter more than the counter algorithm.

Model the budget

  • Scope by authenticated tenant/user/API key before IP where possible. Charge weighted cost for expensive queries instead of pretending every request costs one token.
  • Separate sustained rate, burst capacity and concurrency. Version policy and shadow new rules before enforcement to measure false positives.

Bound distributed error

  • Use atomic server-side updates for a precise shared bucket; use local token leases only when bounded overshoot is acceptable and measurable.
  • Choose fail-open or fail-closed per endpoint. Authentication, checkout and public reads have different abuse risk and availability cost.

Operate the control

  • Track allowed, rejected and shadow decisions by policy version plus limiter latency, hot keys and degraded-mode duration—without putting user IDs in metric labels.
  • Return a stable error contract and Retry-After where meaningful; combine rate limits with quotas, bot controls and origin concurrency protection.

  • (2) make heavy work async (queue + worker) → reduces hot-path latency
  • (3) multi-tier caching + read replicas; (4) bulkhead + circuit breaker + timeout to isolate failures; (5) correctly sized connection pools; (6) backpressure/load shedding under overload

Core idea: separate the hot path from heavy work, 'fail in a controlled way' under overload.

When a dependency or worker queue saturates, how do you set bounded queues, admission or concurrency limits, priorities, and shedding rules—and which signals trigger and reverse them?10-system-design-rate-limit.surviving-high-load.q2
  • Backpressure: signal upstream to slow down (bounded queue, reactive request(n)) instead of absorbing unboundedly and running OOM
  • Load shedding: proactively reject/downgrade lower-priority requests under overload (429/503, a degraded response) to keep the system alive for what matters

Prioritize requests by importance (OTP > marketing).

Bottom line:

a good system 'fails in a controlled way' — better to reject part of the traffic than crash entirely.

▸ DEEP DIVE · SENIOR
  • backpressure = signal upstream to slow down (a full bounded queue blocks, reactive request(n), TCP flow control) instead of absorbing unboundedly → OOM/cascading failure
  • load shedding = reject/downgrade lower-priority requests + admission control (limit incoming concurrency) & a priority queue

Set each bound from the protected resource and the useful deadline:

  • concurrency limit: cap in-flight work per dependency, tenant, and priority class; derive it from measured service time and reserve a critical share.
  • bounded queue: choose capacity from service_rate × useful_wait, not from available memory. Reject or cancel work whose deadline cannot be met, and expose oldest age.
  • retry budget: reserve only a small share of traffic for retries, use exponential backoff with full jitter, and stop at the end-to-end deadline. A retry consumes admission like a new request.
  • priority lanes: keep checkout/OTP capacity separate from bulk work; shed bulk before borrowing the reserved share.

Trigger on sustained queue-oldest-age or deadline misses, in-flight saturation, downstream p99/error/timeout, retry ratio, and rejection rate. CPU is supporting evidence, not the only trigger. Use hysteresis and a gradual drain when reversing the policy (see surviving-high-load.q5).

OTA: under overload → prioritize checkout/payment & OTP over search/marketing; temporarily disable secondary features (recommendations, images) to protect the booking flow. Measure via saturation metrics to trigger early.

Retry and ambiguous outcome:

  • If the limiter allowed POST /booking and the provider timed out, the outcome is unknown: the provider may have committed. A blind retry can double-book or double-charge.
  • Use a stable idempotency key and a status/reconciliation path (17-rest-api-design.contracts-errors-lifecycle.q1). Count each retry against the end-to-end budget; a 429 is retryable only under the caller contract, not because the limiter made the operation safe.
Given measured read/write load and access patterns, which database-scaling step would you choose next, and what metric, failure mode, and exit criterion justify that choice?10-system-design-rate-limit.surviving-high-load.q3

There is no universal database-scaling ladder. Start with the measured bottleneck and the invariant that must remain true; the cheapest change is useful only when it addresses both.

  1. Query and connection shape: inspect plans, indexes, lock time, pool waits, payload size, and transaction scope before adding infrastructure.
  2. Read path: replicas, caches, or a derived read model help only when the read can tolerate their freshness and authorization boundary.
  3. Write path: batching, asynchronous handoff, or a queue can shorten the request path, but they do not erase write volume and introduce ordering, retry, and reconciliation work.
  4. Partition or shard: choose a key that keeps the common invariant local, quantify skew, and provide a migration, rollback, and repair plan before distributing the source of truth.
Bottom line:

For every proposed layer, state the measured constraint, the expected effect, the new failure mode, and the metric that would remove or reverse the change.

▸ DEEP DIVE · SENIOR
  • a table with tens of millions of rows or a primary handling thousands of writes per second is neither automatically healthy nor automatically a reason to shard. Hardware, schema, indexes, durability settings, payloads, and p99 targets decide the useful threshold.
  • replicas introduce lag and read-after-write choices (06-db-scaling.replication-consistency.q2); caches introduce invalidation and origin amplification (→ For a read-heavy service, which caching pattern and…); queues introduce delivery and ordering semantics.
  • OTA search may use a rebuildable read model while booking keeps its inventory and money invariant local. Partition by time only when retention and query patterns support it; shard by a domain key only after proving the key spreads writes and preserves the common transaction.
  • measure each change with a before-and-after workload, error budget, and rollback criterion. Architecture is not a checklist ordered by fashionable components.
An endpoint’s p99 has regressed: how do you use metrics, tracing, profiles, and queue, lock, connection, or GC signals to prove the constraint and select a safe experiment?10-system-design-rate-limit.surviving-high-load.q4

Measure before optimizing. First define the user symptom, the time window, and a baseline; then prove which resource is saturated before changing the design.

  • metrics: request rate, errors, latency percentiles, utilization, saturation, queue age, pool waits, and retry volume
  • traces: compare the critical path with a healthy request and identify the dependency or stage that consumes the budget
  • profiles and runtime evidence: CPU, allocation, lock contention, GC pauses, I/O, network retransmits, and database plans

Little's law is L = lambda × W at steady state: average work in the system equals throughput times average time in the system.

It is a consistency check for measured concurrency and latency, not a standalone sizing promise. Amdahl's law likewise says to improve the part that dominates observed time, not the component that is easiest to discuss.

▸ DEEP DIVE · SENIOR
  • compare before and after a traffic change, deploy, configuration rollout, or dependency incident. A single high CPU graph cannot distinguish useful work from retry storms or lock waits.
  • look for a saturation signal that matches the symptom: pending database connections, oldest queue age, lock wait, CPU run queue, disk latency, GC pause, or a slow remote span.
  • turn the diagnosis into a bounded experiment with a success metric and rollback.
  • if a PSP or GDS span dominates checkout, do not casually cache or parallelize the final money or booking decision; reduce optional work, set deadlines/concurrency limits, and preserve an explicit pending/reconciliation state.

Common mistake: micro-optimizing local code while the bottleneck is a database, network dependency, or an unbounded retry loop.

  • the first-60-seconds host process is in (21-linux-production-debug.diagnosing-the-system.q1); for a JVM suspicion, use a thread dump (21-linux-production-debug.jvm-network-deep-dive-on-prod.q1) before production-safe profiling (21-linux-production-debug.jvm-network-deep-dive-on-prod.q3).
What makes overload control reversible instead of a permanent emergency switch?10-system-design-rate-limit.surviving-high-load.q5

Define overload as a controller with an activation rule, a recovery rule, and hysteresis.

Observe queue depth and age, in-flight concurrency, admission rejections, downstream latency, error and timeout rate, retry amplification, CPU/memory, and the resource that is actually saturated.

Activate bounded admission, priority scheduling, feature degradation, or load shedding when the signal crosses a sustained threshold; do not react to one noisy sample.

Recovery should require a lower threshold held for a window, then ramp traffic gradually. Keep a separate emergency path for critical work, but make its capacity and exhaustion visible.

When the queue is full, fail fast or return a documented pending/retry response; an unbounded queue only moves the outage into latency and memory.

Test the controller with a dependency stall, cache cold start, retry storm, worker crash, and recovery. Record the state transition and reason so operators can tell whether the system is protecting itself or silently losing work.

Bottom line:

Overload policy is part of the product contract: which work is rejected, degraded, delayed, or preserved must be explicit and measurable.
