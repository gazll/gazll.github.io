Scope and problem framing

Accept email, SMS and push intents from product services, respect consent and quiet hours, render versioned templates, schedule or prioritize work, survive provider failures and report an honest delivery state.

Requirements

Functional requirements

  • Accept a notification intent from a producer, deduplicate it, and decide the channels and the send time from the recipient's preferences.
  • Render per channel and per locale, then hand the message to the right provider with the provider's own retry and rate discipline.
  • Consume provider callbacks to reconcile delivery state, and dead-letter what cannot be delivered rather than retrying it forever.
  • Keep transactional messages and campaign messages on separate paths so that a campaign can never delay a password reset.

Quality attributes

  • An intent that was accepted must reach a terminal state — delivered, suppressed or dead-lettered. A notification that quietly vanishes is indistinguishable from one that was never sent.
  • At-most-once matters more than at-least-once here, because the duplicate is visible to a human. Deduplication is a correctness feature, not an optimization.
  • Provider throughput is a hard external limit and must be treated as one. Accepting faster than the provider drains simply moves the queue inside your system.
  • Preference and quiet-hours checks must happen at send time, not at accept time, or a message queued before a user opted out is delivered after they did.

Capacity and constraints

  • Size the intake by peak intent rate with the daily volume beside it: ~25 rps peak (100k notifications/day) at T2, ~250 rps peak (1M/day) at T3, ~25,000 rps peak (100M/day) at T4. Transactional intent follows the application at 3–5×, while a campaign send is a step function that arrives all at once and must be sized as its own event.
  • Intent rate and delivery rate are different numbers and must be sized apart. One intent can fan out to push, email and SMS, so three channels turn 1M intents/day into 3M provider calls/day against three independent rate limits.
  • Provider throughput, not your own capacity, is usually the binding constraint. A push provider may accept thousands per second while an SMS aggregator accepts tens, so the slowest channel sets the drain time for a campaign.
  • Judge queue isolation between transactional and campaign per tier:
    • not needed at T1, where one worker pool drains everything before anyone waits
    • worth it at T2, where a small campaign can already add seconds to a password reset
    • mandatory at T3, because one campaign fills every worker for minutes
    • needs its own scaling story at T4, where per-tenant fairness inside the campaign path becomes its own scheduling problem.
  • Budget retry amplification. Three attempts with backoff against a provider that is failing turns a 1M/day channel into 3M/day at exactly the moment the provider is least able to take it, so the retry budget must be capped globally and not only per message.
  • Size the dead-letter path deliberately:how long failed intents are kept, who is expected to look at them, and what the growth rate is when a provider is down for an hour.

Data model

Compare records by ownership and responsibility before choosing storage. A record is authoritative, operational or derived; mixing those roles creates unclear consistency boundaries.

Record / modelFields, ownership and responsibility

01Intent

producer, event key, recipient, template id, payload reference, priority class and requested send window. The event key is what makes the whole pipeline idempotent for a retrying producer.

02Delivery attempt

intent, channel, provider, attempt number, provider message id, state and the last transition time. One row per attempt, because the audit question is always "what did we actually try".

03Recipient preference

per channel opt-in, quiet hours with a timezone, frequency caps and the effective time of the last change. Timezone-aware, or quiet hours are enforced in the wrong nine hours for half the users.

04Template

id, version, per-locale content and the variables it requires. Versioned, so that a rendered message can be explained months later and a template change cannot rewrite history.

05Suppression list

hard bounces, complaints and unsubscribes with a reason and an effective time. This list overrides preferences and must be consulted on every send, because sending to it is what damages sender reputation.

Compare the models on
  • transaction boundary and invariant
  • read/write path and indexes
  • partition key and hot-key risk
  • retention, audit and privacy
  • whether it can be rebuilt after loss

Technology choices

Treat this as a decision map, not a shopping list. Each component must solve a named constraint and carry an explicit operational cost.

Layer / optionChoice, purpose and boundary

01Intake API writing the intent and…

Intake API writing the intent and an outbox record in one transaction, so an accepted notification cannot be lost between the API and the queue.

02Separate queues by priority class rather…

Separate queues by priority class rather than one queue with a priority field. A field lets a single consumer pool starve one class; separate queues let you give the classes different worker counts and different failure policies.

03Per-channel dispatchers with their own token…

Per-channel dispatchers with their own token buckets sized to the provider's published limits, plus a circuit breaker so a failing provider sheds rather than consumes the retry budget of the whole system.

04Rendering as a separate stage with a bounded cache

templates change rarely and rendering the same campaign body a million times is pure waste.

05Callback consumer reconciling provider state asynchronously, tolerant of out-of-order and duplicate callbacks

every provider sends both, and treating the callback as authoritative without ordering is how a delivered message ends up marked failed.

06Observability

intent-to-delivery latency by priority class, provider acceptance and error rate by code, retry budget consumption, queue depth and oldest-message age per class, suppression growth, and dead-letter age.

Challenge every choice with
  • simplest viable alternative
  • consistency and latency impact
  • failure and degraded mode
  • team/on-call capability
  • migration and exit trigger

Code samples

Idempotent intake

The unique key on (producer, event_key) is the deduplication. A producer that retries the same event gets the original intent back rather than a second notification.

sql
CREATE TABLE intent (
  intent_id     uuid PRIMARY KEY,
  producer      text        NOT NULL,
  event_key     text        NOT NULL,   -- one per business event, not per call
  recipient_id  bigint      NOT NULL,
  template_id   text        NOT NULL,
  priority      text        NOT NULL,   -- transactional | campaign
  send_after    timestamptz,
  created_at    timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
  CONSTRAINT intent_dedup UNIQUE (producer, event_key)
);

INSERT INTO intent (intent_id, producer, event_key, recipient_id, template_id, priority)
VALUES (:id, :producer, :event_key, :recipient, :template, :priority)
ON CONFLICT (producer, event_key) DO NOTHING
RETURNING intent_id;

-- no row returned means we already accepted this event: return the original id, do not resend

Preferences are checked at send, not at accept

Abridged. The ordering is the design: suppression outranks preference, and both are read now rather than at the time the intent was queued.

java
void dispatch(Intent intent) {
    Recipient r = recipients.load(intent.recipientId());

    // suppression is absolute and is checked first: sending to a hard bounce or a
    // complaint is what damages sender reputation for every other message
    if (suppression.contains(r, intent.channel())) {
        intents.terminate(intent, SUPPRESSED);
        return;
    }

    // read now, not at accept time: the user may have opted out while this queued
    Preference p = preferences.current(r, intent.channel());
    if (!p.optedIn()) { intents.terminate(intent, OPTED_OUT); return; }

    if (p.inQuietHours(clock.now(), r.timezone()) && intent.priority() != TRANSACTIONAL) {
        intents.reschedule(intent, p.nextWindow(clock.now(), r.timezone()));
        return;
    }

    rateLimiter.forChannel(intent.channel()).acquire();   // provider limit, not ours
    providers.send(intent, render(intent, r.locale()));
}

Trade-offs

Each row is a tension the design accepts—not a universal best practice. Review both sides and record the condition that would reverse the decision.

01
Deduplicating on a producer-supplied event key…

Deduplicating on a producer-supplied event key makes retries safe and puts the correctness burden on the producer to generate that key once per event rather than once per call. Generating it per call is the single most common way duplicates reach users.

02
Fanning out to all channels at once is fast and noisy; escalating channel by channel is quieter and slower. The choice belongs to the notification type

a security alert and a marketing nudge should not use the same policy.

03
Honouring quiet hours delays delivery and…

Honouring quiet hours delays delivery and can push a batch into the next morning's peak. Either accept the delay and size for the resulting spike, or define which priority classes ignore quiet hours entirely.

04
A shared retry budget protects providers…

A shared retry budget protects providers and means one failing channel can consume the allowance of a healthy one. Per-channel budgets avoid that and let a global provider incident go unnoticed for longer.

05
Storing rendered content makes debugging trivial…

Storing rendered content makes debugging trivial and turns the notification store into a copy of everything you ever sent, including personal data. Store the template version and the variables instead, and re-render on demand.

06
Campaign and transactional traffic on one path is simpler to build and is the failure that produces the worst incident

a marketing send that delays password resets is a support outage caused by a feature nobody considered load-bearing.

Failure review — answers for this design
01

Which invariant can break?

A recipient must receive an accepted notification at most once, and must never receive one they have opted out of or that is on the suppression list. Both break the same way: a check performed at accept time instead of at send time, against state that changed while the message sat in a queue.

02

Which metric detects it first?

Oldest-message age per priority class, not aggregate queue depth. A campaign filling the workers shows as transactional age climbing while total depth looks normal, which is exactly the incident queue isolation exists to prevent.

03

How is the blast radius contained?

Priority classes are separate queues with separate worker pools, each channel has its own token bucket and breaker, and the retry budget is capped globally. A failing provider therefore sheds its own traffic instead of consuming the capacity of healthy channels.

04

Can retry duplicate or reorder work?

Yes, at three points: a producer retry, a queue redelivery and a duplicate provider callback. The first is handled by the event key, the second by the attempt record, and the third by treating callbacks as idempotent state transitions keyed on the provider message id rather than as commands.

05

How do we repair and prove recovery?

Drain the dead-letter queue selectively after re-checking suppression and preferences — replaying it blindly is how users receive a day-old batch at once. Recovery is proven when oldest-message age is back inside budget for every class, provider error rate is at baseline, and a sampled set of intents shows exactly one delivered attempt each.

Engineering deep dives

Asynchronous delivery and replay

Moving work off the request path trades immediate failure for lag, duplicates, ordering constraints and operational backlog.

Define delivery semantics

  • Assume at-least-once at service boundaries. Give each event a stable ID, partition key, schema version and event time; make every side effect idempotent.
  • Guarantee order only within the business entity that needs it. Global order limits parallelism and usually encodes no useful invariant.

Treat lag as user impact

  • Observe oldest-message age, input/output rate, retry count, poison messages and partition skew. Queue depth alone hides slow old work behind new small messages.
  • Use retry topics or delayed queues with expiry and a DLQ; DLQ is not resolution until it has ownership, alerting and a safe replay tool.

Evolve safely

  • Use backward/forward-compatible schemas and tolerant readers. Deploy consumers before producers when adding fields and preserve raw events for bounded replay.
  • Replay into an isolated output or shadow consumer first; throttle it so historical traffic cannot starve live processing or repeat external effects.

Observability and security review

Instrumentation should explain user impact and the responsible dependency without leaking identity or creating unbounded telemetry cost.

Start from user SLIs

  • Define success, latency, freshness and correctness SLIs at the system boundary; alert on error-budget burn, then use dependency metrics for diagnosis.
  • Correlate traces, metrics and structured logs with trace/span IDs. Preserve request, policy and schema versions but redact secrets and sensitive payloads.

Keep telemetry bounded

  • Never use user ID, object ID or raw URL as a metric label. Use logs/traces for high-cardinality detail and explicitly monitor cardinality overflow.
  • Sample by value: retain errors, slow paths and rare business failures at higher rates while keeping predictable storage and query cost.

Review the trust boundary

  • Authorize the object and action on every request, use least-privilege service identities and short-lived credentials, encrypt in transit/at rest and audit privileged changes.
  • Threat-model abuse, replay, enumeration, tenant crossover and data retention. Security controls need degraded behavior and recovery drills like every other dependency.

Design a notification platform for OTP, transactional, and marketing messages. Define acceptance and delivery semantics, preference enforcement, deduplication, retries and DLQ ownership, provider failover, and delivery-status trust.11-system-design-cases.the-big-prompts.q2

Define the contract before adding queues: accepted means the caller was authorized, policy-checked, deduplicated/idempotently recorded, and the notification request was durably stored or queued. It does not mean delivered.

Track states such as accepted → queued → sent-to-provider → provider-accepted → provider-confirmed / failed / expired. A provider callback may prove provider or carrier handling, but usually cannot prove a user read the message.

Flow: producer → idempotent ingestion → policy and preference router → priority channel queues → workers → provider adapters → status store and DLQ.

OTP:

OTP should have a priority lane, a short deadline, and bounded retries. Retrying after its code expires is usually worse than failing fast and asking the user to request a new code.

▸ DEEP DIVE · SENIOR
  • enforce consent, channel preference, quiet hours, unsubscribe, suppression, and per-user limits again at send time; an opt-out after enqueue must still win.
  • distinguish ingestion idempotency, semantic deduplication window, and provider idempotency. If the first provider outcome is unknown, failover can create a duplicate; query status or wait for a callback when possible.
  • APNs and FCM have provider-specific token lifecycle, TTL/expiry, and collapse/coalescing behavior. Provider acceptance or a callback is still provider-side evidence: persist the provider message ID, update invalid-token state, and do not retry a dead token forever.
  • for critical state changes, keep a durable in-app inbox/read model and let clients sync; push is a wake-up hint that may be delayed, collapsed, reordered, or expire. Do not use collapse/TTL for OTP or a unique state transition without an explicit product contract.
  • sign and verify callbacks, deduplicate them, and make state transitions tolerant of duplicate or out-of-order events.
  • DLQ is a quarantine with an owner, alerting, remediation reason, expiry policy, and controlled replay; it is not an invisible infinite retry queue.
  • expose delivery semantics honestly: transactional messages are high priority and monitored, while marketing is best-effort and may be shed under overload.

Case metadata:

  • Workload: OTP, transactional and marketing rates, payload size, channel/provider mix and per-tenant burst
  • Freshness: acceptance is synchronous but provider delivery/read status is asynchronous and bounded by its contract
  • Duplicate tolerance: ingestion key, semantic dedup window and provider message ID
  • Recovery owner: notification router, provider adapter and DLQ owner
